> Method note: I did this research with web search only. ## Q1. Premise vs. topic vs. attitude: the core models (Dean, Carter, Vorhaus, Kaplan, Perret, Corley, incongruity theory, benign violation)

### Takeaway
Every school of thought separates the topic (the subject, e.g. "airlines") from the premise (a specific, opinionated, not-yet-funny claim about it, e.g. "airlines treat you like cargo that pays"). The punchline mechanism is the same in all of them: the setup creates one interpretation, and the punch forces a second, hidden interpretation (Dean's "two stories", Suls's incongruity-resolution). Benign violation theory adds the emotional condition: the shift must feel wrong and OK at the same time.

### Cited Findings
**Greg Dean (Step by Step to Stand-Up Comedy; also "The NLP of Comedy")**
- Dean's joke anatomy: Setup → (Target) Assumption → Connector → Reinterpretation → Punch. The method is to list what the setup implies, pick one assumption to target, then find a reinterpretation that shatters it. Canonical example: "For Father's Day I took my father out…" The audience assumes "out to dinner", and the punch reveals that "out" meant something else (in the worked example, the father was the dinner). — [Juggle / Scotty Meltzer on Dean, via search summary](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/); [Dean, "The NLP of Comedy" PDF](https://theletterworthpress.org/nlpworld/4-1DEAN.PDF)
- The **target assumption** is the one the punch directly shatters. The **reinterpretation** is the new meaning that becomes the core of the "second story", and it is usually *not* the punch itself. The punch is the line that reveals it. — [Scribd study summary of Dean's "Secret of a Joke"](https://www.scribd.com/doc/56164449/Secret-of-a-Joke)
- Dean's tightness advice: target a specific person, show or politician rather than a group or genre, and give only the minimum information needed for the audience to jump from assumption to shatter. — [Juggle, via search summary](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/)
- The **connector** is the element (often a word or phrase with two meanings, like "out" above) that supports both the first and second interpretation. This is the "connector/two-meanings" model. — [Juggle, via search summary](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/)

**Judy Carter (The Comedy Bible / The New Comedy Bible)**
- The four core "attitude" words are **weird, scary, hard, stupid**. Workbook checklists also include "bugs me". The writer asks "What's [attitude] about [topic]?" — [bigbencomedy notes on The Comedy Bible](https://www.bigbencomedy.com/archives/tag/comedy-bible); [sobrief summary](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/the-new-comedy-bible)
- The **premise** is the answer to the attitude-plus-topic question: an insightful, original, specific observation that is *not yet funny*. Example: "What's weird about body piercing?" gives the premise "Piercing is stupid. It's painful enough just to be in a relationship." — [sobrief summary](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/the-new-comedy-bible)
- Carter's structure (summaries disagree on order): Topic + Attitude + Premise as the setup, then a payoff of **Act-out**, **Mix** (combining the premise with an unrelated element or person) or **Turn**. Another summary gives Attitude + Topic + Premise + Act-out + Mix + Act-out. — [sobrief](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/the-new-comedy-bible); [befreed summary](https://www.befreed.ai/book/the-comedy-bible-by-judy-carter)

**John Vorhaus (The Comic Toolbox, 1994)**
- "Comedy = truth + pain." Every human moment contains an underlying truth and pain, and a joke acknowledges both indirectly. Example: a man falling past floors of a skyscraper says "so far, so good." The truth is that he is in peril, the pain is that he is helpless, and the line acknowledges both sideways. — [Film Courage interview with Vorhaus](https://filmcourage.com/2024/03/21/the-comic-toolbox-how-to-be-funny-even-if-youre-not-john-vorhaus-full-interview); [Silman-James publisher page](https://www.silmanjamespress.com/catalogs/the-comic-toolbox)
- The truth should speak directly to the audience's experience, and the pain is exaggerated to create the comedy. — [Key Takeaways blog](https://theresearching.blogspot.com/2017/10/key-takeaways-from-comic-toolbox-by.html)
- The core tools include **Clash of Context**, Tension and Release, the Law of Comic Opposites, the Wildly Inappropriate Response, and the "Myth of the Last Great Idea" (generate many options instead of protecting one). — [Stafforini book summary](https://stafforini.com/works/vorhaus-1994-comic-toolbox-how/)
- **Clash of context** means taking something out of its usual place and putting it where it doesn't fit, a forced union of incompatibles (e.g. a lunar golf course, Madonna singing opera). — [Key Takeaways blog](https://theresearching.blogspot.com/2017/10/key-takeaways-from-comic-toolbox-by.html); [Jacqui Murray blog](https://jacquimurray.net/2025/04/25/how-to-be-funny-if-youre-not-ii/)

**Steve Kaplan (The Hidden Tools of Comedy, 2013)**
- Kaplan's comic equation centers on the **Non-Hero**: an ordinary person battling insurmountable odds without the tools or talents to win, but never giving up hope. The non-hero is "not an idiot," just someone who *lacks something*. Tool #1 is "Winning" (what the character wants), and Tool #2 is the Non-Hero. — [The Story Department](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-1/); [The Story Department pt. 2](https://thestorydepartment.com/the-hidden-tools-of-comedy-2/)
- The non-hero doesn't try to be funny, he just doesn't know. Laughs come from his being a beat behind what the audience has already figured out, and from struggling, failing and not quitting. — [Goodreads reader notes](https://www.goodreads.com/notes/19228619-the-hidden-tools-of-comedy/843770-kmo); [Clownlink review](https://clownlink.com/?p=4026)

**Jerry Corley / Gene Perret**
- Corley (Tonight Show/Leno writer for 8 years, wrote for Chris Rock and Bill Hicks) teaches that the key is understanding *why* people laugh. He calls analogies "almost instant jokes". — [Toastmasters "Cracking the Laughter Code"](https://www.toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2018/aug2018/cracking-the-laughter-code); [funnyhow Substack](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/begin-with-a-frame)
- I found no reliably attributed Perret-specific formulation. See Gaps.

**Academic theory**
- **Incongruity-resolution (Suls 1972, two-stage model):** Stage 1, the punchline creates an incongruity (an ending that doesn't seem to follow from the setup). Stage 2, the listener problem-solves to find a cognitive rule that makes the incongruity make sense. Resolution does not erase the incongruity, and both coexist. — [Ritchie / core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/429710601.pdf); [SAGE Encyclopedia of Humor Studies](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/encyclopedia-of-humor-studies/chpt/cognitive-aspects)
- Neural support: left hemisphere contributes to incongruity detection, right hemisphere to resolution and appreciation. — [Marinkovic 2011](https://stbil.sdsu.edu/pdfs/Marinkovic-2011.pdf)
- Ritchie notes that incongruity-resolution has "no precise statement" and authors disagree on details. — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/429710601.pdf)
- **Benign Violation Theory (McGraw & Warren):** humor arises when (1) something is a violation (of a moral, social or physical norm), (2) it is appraised as benign, and (3) both appraisals happen simultaneously. A violation becomes benign through weak commitment to the norm, psychological distance, or an alternative interpretation (play-fighting, tickling). — [petermcgraw.org](https://petermcgraw.org/?p=400); [McGraw & Warren 2010, Psychological Science 21:1141–1149](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf)
- Warren & McGraw (2016, JPSP 110(3):407–430) argue that "incongruity" is defined inconsistently and that benign violation better separates humorous from non-humorous stimuli. — [JPSP 2016](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jpspy/pdf/10.1037/pspi0000041~differentiating-what-is-humorous-from-what-is-not)
- Critique: one theory is unlikely to cover all humor, from slapstick to knock-knock jokes. — [NPR, Tania Lombrozo](https://www.npr.org/sections/13.7/2016/04/25/475622815/hey-what-are-you-laughing-at)

### Inferences
- **Working vocabulary for analysis.** These terms (my own synthesis) can be used consistently across the four comics:
  - **Topic**: the noun ("my dad," "the military," "disabled people").
  - **Attitude**: the emotional stance (Carter: weird, scary, hard, stupid, bugs me).
  - **Premise**: a specific, arguable, not-yet-funny claim ("My dad treats every purchase as a hostage negotiation").
  - **Setup**: the lines that build the target assumption.
  - **Connector**: the ambiguous hinge.
  - **Reveal/punch**: the word or phrase that flips it.
  - **Tags**: extra punches on the same reinterpretation.
  - **Act-out**: dramatizing the premise.
  - **Callback**: a later return to an earlier reveal.
- Dean's and Suls's models are the same idea in different language: setup = Story 1 / incongruity primed, reinterpretation = cognitive rule / resolution. Benign violation explains *whether the audience laughs or gasps* once the flip lands. That matters most for Gervais and Gillis, whose material lives at the edge of "violation".
- **Mapping to the four comics (hypotheses to test against transcripts):**
  - **Jimmy O. Yang**: a Carter-style attitude + act-out engine. Topic is immigrant parents or dating, attitude is "weird/hard," and the payoff is very often an act-out of his dad's voice. A Vorhaus truth + pain fit: the pain of not meeting parental expectations.
  - **Ricky Gervais**: built on benign-violation management. He picks a taboo *subject*, frames a different *target* (often himself, the audience's assumptions, or the powerful), and uses irony as the "alternative interpretation" route to benign.
  - **Louis C.K.**: Vorhaus truth + pain in its purest form. He is also a Kaplan non-hero, a self-loathing loser who confesses a monstrous thought, which is a violation made benign by self-implication.
  - **Shane Gillis**: character/persona act-outs (his dad, Trump impression, military, Pennsylvania guys) and a Kaplan non-hero persona ("dumb guy" who is a beat behind). Benign distance comes from playing the character rather than stating the view.

### Gaps
- I could not access the primary texts of Dean, Carter, Vorhaus or Kaplan in this session. Exact wording, chapter numbering and the order of Carter's formula steps are reported by secondary summaries that disagree with each other.
- I found no reliably sourced summary of Gene Perret's specific teaching (his books include "Comedy Writing Step by Step"). No Jerry Corley joke formula was found.

## Q2. Punchline mechanics: misdirection, reveal at the end, hard word last, rule of three, tags, act-outs, callbacks, toppers, mixes, comparisons, escalation, specificity, economy

### Takeaway
The punch is a reveal that should come as late as possible, ideally on the last word. Everything before it should be minimal and should build the false assumption. After the reveal, good comics milk the same premise with tags, act-outs, escalation and callbacks, so one premise yields several laughs.

### Cited Findings
- **Punch word last.** Put the punch as close to the end as possible, because the punch word triggers the laugh. Don't add words after the reveal. — [Toastmasters, July 2018 "Humor"](https://toastmasters.org/magazine/magazine-issues/2018/july2018/humor); [helpingyouharmonise "Don't Tread on Your Punchlines"](https://www.helpingyouharmonise.com/punchlines)
- If you keep talking once the crowd starts laughing, you train them to stop laughing so they can hear you. — [helpingyouharmonise](https://www.helpingyouharmonise.com/punchlines)
- **Misdirection** is the Dean mechanism of building a target assumption and then shattering it with a reinterpretation, using a connector that supports both readings. — [Juggle / Dean](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/)
- **Economy (Seinfeld).** In the NYT "How to Write a Joke" video (2012), Seinfeld dissects his Pop-Tart bit, which he says took about two years ("usually I'd write a bit in a couple days"). He writes longhand on legal pads and calls comedy writing "a secretive thing." — [Salon](https://www.salon.com/2012/12/20/jerry_seinfeld_explains_how_he_writes_comedy); [Amara transcript page of the NYT video](https://amara.org/videos/tKpdTlvyHEA2/info/jerry-seinfeld-interview-how-to-write-a-joke-the-new-york-times); [HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/jerry-seinfeld-explains-how-he-writes-jokes_n_2338980/amp)
- Seinfeld revisited and finished the Pop-Tart bit in "23 Hours to Kill" (Netflix, 2020). — [Joe Toplyn](https://joetoplyn.com/how-jerry-seinfeld-writes-a-joke-revealed/)
- **Tags.** A tag is an extra punchline added after the main one on the same setup. Good comics get three, four or five laughs from one premise this way. "Toppers" are sometimes treated as separate from tags (a topper beats the previous laugh) and sometimes as synonyms. — [zeroes "Anatomy of a joke"](https://zeroes.pages.dev/c/comedy/anatomy-of-a-joke); [Wikipedia (mirror) "Stand-up comedy"](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20220416011618/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_comedy)
- **Act-outs.** Jason Zinoman defines an act-out as the comic shifting from narrating to acting the scene. If a punchline isn't landing, acting it out can rescue it. Act-outs need not be big impressions, and a deadpan act-out also counts. — [funnyhow Substack "The power of act outs"](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/the-power-of-act-outs)
- **Mix** (Carter): combine the premise with an unrelated person, situation or voice (e.g. "what if my mom were a drill sergeant"). — [sobrief summary](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/the-new-comedy-bible)
- **Comparisons and analogies** are "almost instant jokes" (Corley). — [funnyhow Substack](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/begin-with-a-frame)
- **Clash of context / wildly inappropriate response** (Vorhaus) are the punch mechanisms for absurdist or escalation jokes. — [Stafforini](https://stafforini.com/works/vorhaus-1994-comic-toolbox-how/)
- **Callbacks** work "horizontally" across a set: a later reference to an earlier joke that rewards attention and builds structure. LLMs struggle with them because they need an overall narrative structure. — [Comedy as architecture](https://data-journalist-agent.github.io/pudding/02_stand-up/blog_opus47_0503_0049/viewer.html); [heise on DeepMind comedian study](https://heise.de/en/news/Stand-up-comedy-LLMs-don-t-write-good-jokes-9778539.html)
- **Pause** before the punch to build anticipation. — [helpingyouharmonise](https://www.helpingyouharmonise.com/punchlines)
- **Specificity**: Dean says to aim at a specific named target, not a genre or group. — [Juggle / Dean](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/)
- **Escalation** fits Kaplan's non-hero, who keeps trying and failing without giving up, so each beat raises the stakes. — [Clownlink review of Kaplan](https://clownlink.com/?p=4026)

### Inferences
- **Rule of three** (list two "normal" items to set a pattern, break it with the third) is a pattern-and-violate application of Dean's target assumption. The first two items create the assumption that the list is consistent. I found no single primary source for it this session, but it is universal in craft books (Vorhaus lists it, per the assignment brief).
- **Hard word last** combines two pieces of craft lore: the reveal word ends the line, and plosive or "k" sounds are said to be funnier (a Neil Simon "Sunshine Boys" line). I did not verify the "k sounds" claim this session, so treat it as folklore.
- **Diagnostic checklist for analyzing a bit:**
  1. Where is the target assumption?
  2. What is the connector?
  3. Is the reveal word last?
  4. How many tags follow?
  5. Is there an act-out, and whose voice is it?
  6. Does anything get called back later?
- **Style hypotheses:**
  - **Louis C.K.**: long escalations ("and then… and then…"), with tags that get progressively darker. He often ends on a blunt hard word ("…and that's when I knew I was a piece of shit").
  - **Gervais**: frequent "meta-tags", where he comments on the audience's reaction to the joke, turning the gasp itself into a second laugh.
  - **Yang and Gillis**: rely heavily on act-outs, with Yang's dad's accent and Gillis's characters and impressions.

### Gaps
- No source found for Gene Perret's specific punchline rules or for the "k sound" folklore.
- I could not fetch Seinfeld's actual video transcript. The commonly repeated details about him cutting syllables and "bridging" bits are not verified here.

## Q3. How working comics generate premises and test and refine them

### Takeaway
Premises come from honest personal pain, annoyance and noticed contradictions, filtered through a consistent persona and point of view. The material is then built on stage over months or years: record every set, listen back, cut filler, rerun. Some comics (Carlin, Louis C.K.) discard each hour once it is recorded, to force new writing.

### Cited Findings
- **Vorhaus truth + pain** as the generation engine: find the true thing and the painful thing in a situation. — [Film Courage](https://filmcourage.com/2024/03/21/the-comic-toolbox-how-to-be-funny-even-if-youre-not-john-vorhaus-full-interview)
- **Carter**: answer "what's weird, scary, hard or stupid about X?" to get premises. — [bigbencomedy](https://www.bigbencomedy.com/archives/tag/comedy-bible)
- **"Talk about what drives you crazy"** is a standard premise-generation prompt. — [funnyhow Substack](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/talk-about-what-drives-you-crazy)
- **Loose premises become A material** through repeated stage work. — [funnyhow Substack](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/how-loose-premises-become-a-material)
- **Louis C.K. / Carlin "throw it out" method.** At a 2010 Carlin tribute, C.K. said that after about 15 years of doing the same hour he adopted Carlin's practice: record a special each year, throw out the material, start over. C.K. called this "inviting the awful". The topics may recur (sex, parenting, his own failings), but the material is new. — [Cal Newport](https://calnewport.com/how-louis-c-k-became-funny-and-why-it-matters/); [funnyhow Substack](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/louis-ck-on-how-george-carlin-inspired); [HuffPost](https://m.huffpost.com/us/entry/950134?m=false). A reader comment disputes that Carlin literally threw material away, which is unverified.
- **Louis C.K.** explained why he throws out jokes at Just For Laughs. — [HuffPost](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/louis-ck-just-for-laughs-throw-out-jokes_n_916250)
- **Jimmy O. Yang**: finds comedy in things his parents say that *they* think are ordinary ("Sometimes my parents say things they think are ordinary, but I catch the humor in them"). Even after 15 years he still drops in at open mics to try new jokes. — [GQ Hong Kong](https://gqhongkong.com/en?p=6887)
- Yang says much of his material is about disappointing his parents, and his dad jokes are among his most popular. — [WIRED via Dailymotion](https://www.dailymotion.com/video/x99jk98)
- Reviewers describe Yang's special as "hot takes on everyday situations like dating, apartment hunting, and (not) living up to parents' expectations". — [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/jimmy-o-yang-good-deal-stand-up-amazon-special/)
- **Shane Gillis**: the core craft is finding what you think is funny and working out how to make everyone else see why it's funny. His writing partner John McKeever often expands a premise Gillis used in stand-up into a sketch. — [RVA Magazine interview](https://rvamag.com/art/comedy/finding-his-way-an-interview-with-shane-gillis.html)
- McKeever (Tires co-writer) says characters must be sympathetic enough that the audience grants forgiveness for edgier jokes, and that a show about jerks voicing bigoted views won't work. — [Hollywood Reporter](https://www.hollywoodreporter.com/tv/tv-features/steve-gerben-tires-season-2-dads-real-tire-shop-mckeever-1236255895/)
- **Ricky Gervais**: most offence comes from confusing a joke's *subject* with its *target*. He wants to be able to justify every joke "by making sure that the target is fair", and he goes into taboo subjects to take the audience somewhere it hasn't been. — [The Spectator interview](https://www.spectator.com.au/2019/12/comedy-in-the-era-of-twitter-outrage-an-interview-with-ricky-gervais/); [NPR 2016](https://www.npr.org/2016/05/01/476078487/ricky-gervais-on-controversial-jokes-celebrities-and-special-correspondents)
- **Testing.** Gary Gulman's first tip is "record every set", and the hard part is listening back and transcribing. — [Gulman tips doc](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1TaM56etMb9ohry8voaLvWf1-FA_DczKdOOcKIJQVh-0/mobilebasic); [funnyhow Substack on Gulman](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/embrace-im-not-for-everyone-and-other)
- Isabel Hagen records sets on her phone, listens later for what worked, and reruns the jokes with notes. — [funnyhow "Tape and listen to every set"](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/tape-and-listen-to-every-set)
- Listening back exposes energy drops, talking too long between laughs, and filler ("ahms, y'knows"). One guide suggests writing 20 minutes for a 10-minute set and cutting the weakest half. Specials often follow 1–3 years of development. These are single, low-authority sources. — [altcomedy workshop guide](https://altcomedy-com.pages.dev/how-to-workshop-material.html)
- Seinfeld spent about two years on one bit (Pop-Tart). — [Salon](https://www.salon.com/2012/12/20/jerry_seinfeld_explains_how_he_writes_comedy)

### Inferences
- **Premise generators used by pros:**
  1. Pain or annoyance (Vorhaus, Carter "bugs me").
  2. "Something only I believe" or an unpopular opinion. This is C.K.'s "of course… but maybe" structure, and Gervais's contrarian takes on religion and offence.
  3. Contradictions noticed in daily life (Seinfeld-style observation).
  4. A persona-specific lens: Yang as the son of Chinese immigrants who failed their expectations, Gillis as a Pennsylvania "dumb jock" type, C.K. as a self-loathing dad, Gervais as the smug atheist provocateur who is the butt of his own arrogance.
- The subject-vs-target distinction (Gervais) and the sympathetic-character rule (McKeever) are practical tools for keeping a violation benign.

### Gaps
- No primary-source account was found of Gillis's or Gervais's day-to-day drafting routine, or of Yang's page-to-stage process beyond open-mic testing.
- The "what do you believe that others don't" prompt is widespread, but I could not attribute it to a specific source this session.

## Q4. Why AI and beginner comedy is often unfunny, and heuristics to avoid it

### Takeaway
LLM and beginner jokes fail because they are generic, safe, repetitive and structurally telegraphed. They lack lived experience, a point of view, real surprise and set-level architecture. The fixes are specificity, a committed opinion, a hidden second meaning, the reveal last, and personal truth + pain.

### Cited Findings
- **DeepMind comedian study.** Twenty comedians tested ChatGPT and Gemini. Output was bland, inoffensive, "rarely offered a surprise", and avoided dark or edgy material. Comedians named the lack of personal experience as a fundamental limitation, and said the models struggled to build interconnected jokes across a set. — [heise](https://heise.de/en/news/Stand-up-comedy-LLMs-don-t-write-good-jokes-9778539.html); [Business Insider NL](https://businessinsider.nl/deepmind-researchers-realize-ai-is-really-really-unfunny-thats-a-problem/)
- **Repetition.** Jentzsch & Kersting found over 90% of 1,008 ChatGPT-generated jokes were variations of the same 25 jokes. — [Yahoo Tech](https://tech.yahoo.com/science/articles/chatgpt-only-tell-25-jokes-104500199.html); [systems-analysis summary "ChatGPT is Fun, but Not Funny"](https://systems-analysis.ru/en/video/chatgpt-humor?t=0)
- **Puns.** LLMs mimic pun structure but fail to notice when a tweaked pun has lost its double meaning. That is, they copy the form without the connector (Dean's term). — [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/computing/chatgpt-and-gemini-can-crack-jokes-but-they-dont-quite-get-your-puns/)
- Benign violation requires a real violation. Material that avoids all violation fails condition 1. — [petermcgraw.org](https://petermcgraw.org/?p=400)
- Dean: specific targets over genres and groups. — [Juggle / Dean](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-you-punch-like-a-girl/)
- Carter: a premise must be specific and original *before* it is funny. — [sobrief](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/the-new-comedy-bible)

### Inferences
- **Concrete anti-hack heuristics (synthesis):**
  1. **Start from a premise, not a topic.** If your opener could be a Wikipedia heading ("Airline food"), you have a topic. Write a one-sentence arguable opinion first.
  2. **Ban puns unless the connector creates a real second story.** Wordplay without a world-view is "hack".
  3. **Write the false assumption on purpose.** List the setup's assumptions (Dean) and choose the least obvious reinterpretation. If the audience can say the punch before you, it is telegraphed.
  4. **The reveal word goes last.** Cut every word after it, and cut setup words that don't build the assumption.
  5. **Specific nouns** (brand names, ages, places, real phrases a parent said) beat generic ones ("a store," "my friend").
  6. **Commit to a POV and allow a real violation**, with a benign route: self-implication (C.K.), irony and fair target (Gervais), a sympathetic character voice (Gillis), affectionate act-out (Yang).
  7. **Use truth + pain.** If nothing is at stake for the speaker, there is no comic tension.
  8. **Get several laughs per premise.** Tags, act-outs and escalation, not one-and-done.
  9. **Architecture.** Plan callbacks and a throughline.
  10. **Avoid stock forms** ("What's the deal with…", "My wife is so X that…", "I'm not saying X, but…", "A guy walks into a bar") unless subverting them.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the DeepMind paper itself. Its exact title and scope vary across write-ups.
- No controlled study was found on beginner (human) comedy failure modes. That section rests on craft-book advice.

## Q5. Laugh lines in long bits vs. one-liner punchlines (laughs-per-minute, multiple laughs within a premise)

### Takeaway
One-liners carry one premise and one punch per joke. Long-form bits stretch one premise across many laugh lines: setup punches, tags, act-outs, escalations and callbacks. Club comics aim for roughly 4–6 laughs per minute, and storytellers trade some frequency for bigger, built payoffs.

### Cited Findings
- Typical laughs per minute (LPM) for a club set is about 4–6 (Wikipedia, MasterClass). One practitioner says 3–4. Phyllis Diller is reportedly the record holder at about 12 LPM. — [Wikipedia mirror](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20220320035739/https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand-up_comedy); [MasterClass](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/how-to-write-stand-up-comedy-in-6-easy-steps); [Hacker News comment](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=32424228)
- Tags let one premise yield 3–5 laughs. — [zeroes "Anatomy of a joke"](https://zeroes.pages.dev/c/comedy/anatomy-of-a-joke)
- In stand-up, "the game is usually a premise", and every observation should add a new angle on the same premise. — [funnyhow "Begin with a frame"](https://funnyhow.substack.com/p/begin-with-a-frame)
- Callbacks give a set horizontal structure, and LLMs lack the narrative structure for this. — [Comedy as architecture](https://data-journalist-agent.github.io/pudding/02_stand-up/blog_opus47_0503_0049/viewer.html); [heise](https://heise.de/en/news/Stand-up-comedy-LLMs-don-t-write-good-jokes-9778539.html)
- Listening back shows "where you're talking too much between laughs", which is the main way to raise the LPM of a long bit. — [altcomedy workshop guide](https://altcomedy-com.pages.dev/how-to-workshop-material.html)

### Inferences
- **Anatomy of a long bit (synthesis):**
  1. **Premise statement**, sometimes with an "attitude laugh" on its own.
  2. **First reveal.**
  3. **Tags and act-outs** on that reveal.
  4. **Escalation**, a new angle on the same premise, each with its own reveal.
  5. **Peak or "button"**, the hardest laugh, often a dark turn or a callback to the premise.
- In a long bit, a *laugh line* is any line that triggers a laugh, including setup lines that are funny in themselves ("setup laughs"). A *punchline* is specifically the reveal that resolves a setup's misdirection.
- **Style mapping:**
  - **Gillis**: long, loose, story-and-character bits with high act-out density.
  - **C.K.**: long-escalation premises with dark buttons.
  - **Gervais**: conversational, digressive, essay-style bits with fewer but bigger laughs, plus meta-commentary tags.
  - **Yang**: tighter setup-punch-act-out cycles inside family stories.
  - These are hypotheses to check by timestamping laughs in their specials, not sourced findings.

### Gaps
- No rigorous measured LPM data for these four comics was found. Laugh counts would need to be made from special recordings.
- The 4–6 LPM benchmark comes from secondary sources (Wikipedia, MasterClass), not a study.
